Work & Employment

Should companies reserve senior posts for women?

opinion

Prompt

Most high-level positions in companies are filled by men even though the workforce in many developed countries is more than 50 per cent female. Companies should be required to allocate a certain percentage of these positions to women. To what extent do you agree?

Model answer

Men still occupy most senior positions in many companies, even where women form a large part of the workforce. Some people propose fixed quotas for women in leadership. I support temporary targets and transparent selection, but not automatic promotion based only on gender.

The case for intervention is strong. Senior appointments often depend on informal networks, previous opportunities and assumptions about who looks like a leader. Women may also receive fewer high-profile assignments or face career penalties for caring responsibilities. If companies simply wait for the imbalance to correct itself without setting any measurable goal, progress can remain extremely slow, sometimes across an entire career. Clear targets can force organisations to examine hidden barriers that a purely voluntary approach allows them to overlook indefinitely.

Rigid quotas, however, carry a cost of their own. When a fixed percentage is imposed, colleagues may assume that a woman was appointed to meet the figure rather than on her record, which weakens the authority she needs to do the job well. It is more effective to change the process that produces the shortlist: insisting that every list for a senior post contains qualified women, publishing promotion and pay data, and making sure that talented staff are given the high-profile projects from which future directors are chosen. Norway's forty per cent board quota, for example, quickly raised the number of women on boards but left the executive ranks below them almost unchanged, which shows that a headline figure is a poor guide to whether a company reflects genuine equality of opportunity.

In conclusion, companies should take active steps to correct unequal opportunity, but leadership decisions must remain evidence-based. Time-limited targets combined with transparent recruitment can improve representation without lowering standards.

Key words and phrases

force organisations to examine hidden barriers
Make employers investigate unnoticed obstacles to fairness.
reflects genuine equality of opportunity
Shows that people truly receive equal chances.
improve representation without lowering standards
Include more groups while maintaining required ability.
