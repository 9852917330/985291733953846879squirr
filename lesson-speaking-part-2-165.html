Situation

A situation where you have to be polite

Part 2

Prompt

Describe a situation where you have to be polite

You should say: - What it is. - What you do to be polite. - Why you have to be polite. - and how you felt about it.

Simple Model Answer

A situation where I needed to remain polite was a university group assignment for Economic Studies. We worked together for about four months. One member was difficult because he often acted as if he knew everything and argued over small details.

At first, his attitude annoyed the rest of us and made meetings longer than necessary. However, our final grade depended on working as a group, so arguing back would not have helped. I tried to stay calm, listen to his ideas and explain disagreements respectfully. When he suggested something useful, I made sure to recognise it.

This was not easy because I sometimes wanted to interrupt or complain. Gradually, though, he became more willing to cooperate. By the end, our discussions were more productive. The experience taught me that politeness can be practical, not just a matter of manners. I cannot always choose the people I work with, but I can control how I respond. Staying calm gave us a better chance of completing the assignment than turning every disagreement into a personal argument.

Advanced Model Answer

I always try to be polite in most situations, that's just how I was brought up, but I'll admit there are some people out there who genuinely seem to enjoy feeding on negative energy, and this is a story about one of them.

It happened when I had to work in a team with some university classmates on an assignment for Economic Studies. There was one guy in our group who I really couldn't stand, he always had half-baked knowledge about everything but acted like he knew it all, and his attitude wound the rest of us up constantly. Meetings would drag on because he'd argue over small details, and the whole project felt a lot more stressful than it needed to be.

The reason I had to be polite was simple: we were stuck working together for about four months, and getting a good grade depended on the group actually functioning. So instead of arguing back, I made a point of staying calm in meetings, actually listening to his ideas before disagreeing, and giving him credit whenever he did contribute something useful, even when it was hard to bite my tongue. It was difficult at first, mainly because he always assumed he was right, but by sticking with that approach, things gradually improved.

By the end of the project he wasn't nearly as difficult as he'd been at the start, and he'd actually become a lot more willing to collaborate with the rest of us. That experience taught me that you can't shield yourself from negative people completely, so the best you can do is learn how to handle them calmly and grow a slightly thicker skin.
