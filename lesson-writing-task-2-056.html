Education

Should sport be compulsory at school?

Discussion

Prompt

Some people believe that sport is an essential part of school life for children, while others feel it should be purely optional. Discuss these opposing views and give your own opinion.

Model answer

The place of sport in the school curriculum is a subject of real disagreement, and there are reasonable arguments on both sides, although in my view the case for making it compulsory is the stronger one. On the one hand, some people feel that sport uses up time which could be better spent on academic subjects such as exam preparation.

Critics also argue that it is unwise to force children into activities they have no interest in. A pupil who dreads every games lesson learns little from it, and some youngsters are simply not sporty and would benefit more from extra study. A third concern is the conduct of professional sport itself: doping and corruption scandals have become common in cycling and athletics, and some parents feel that these set a poor example to young people.

On the other hand, the clearest benefit of sport is its contribution to children's physical development at the stage when habits are formed. Children who never exercise at school are far less likely to do so as adults, and obesity among young people has risen sharply in countries where physical education has been cut. Poor fitness also affects concentration, so the time given to sport is partly repaid in the classroom.

Supporters of school sport also emphasise the value of team games such as football or hockey, in which children must accept instruction, depend on others and put a shared aim above their own performance. A related argument is that a competitive spirit, so important today, is best formed early: learning to lose a match and return to training the following week teaches a resilience that examinations alone rarely develop.

Overall, I believe that sport should be compulsory for school children, because the positive effect on their mental and physical development outweighs the objections. Schools should, however, offer a range wide enough for every child to find something they enjoy, and should teach fair play as firmly as they teach the game itself.
