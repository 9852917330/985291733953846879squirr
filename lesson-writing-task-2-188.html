Work & Employment

Competing with younger people for the same jobs

problem

Prompt

Nowadays more and more people have to compete with young people for the same jobs. What problems does this cause? What are some possible solutions?

Model answer

Older workers increasingly compete with younger applicants for the same jobs. This can create longer unemployment, resentment and a loss of confidence for both groups. The best response is not to favour one age group, but to improve training and make recruitment more transparent.

Young applicants may struggle to gain their first experience because employers prefer candidates who can work immediately. Older applicants, meanwhile, may face assumptions that their skills are outdated or that they will be expensive to employ. When both groups are rejected repeatedly, the result can be long-term exclusion from the labour market.

Governments should fund practical retraining linked to real vacancies, while companies should describe the skills required rather than asking for a narrow age profile. Paid internships can help younger people enter a field, and short conversion courses can help experienced workers update digital or technical skills. Mixed-age teams should also be encouraged because they combine recent training with practical judgement.

There is also a direct financial case for retaining experienced staff rather than replacing them wholesale. Recruiting and training a new employee typically costs several months of salary before that person becomes fully productive, so losing a worker's accumulated knowledge of clients, systems and established professional networks is expensive to replace, not merely inconvenient. A structured handover, in which an employee nearing retirement mentors a newer colleague for several months rather than leaving on a fixed date, protects this knowledge while still opening a genuine route into the profession for someone younger. Public employment services could support such arrangements directly, by funding the mentoring period itself rather than offering generic courses with little labour-market value.

In conclusion, age competition becomes harmful when employers rely on stereotypes instead of evidence. Skills-based recruitment, accessible training and flexible career routes can give both generations a fair chance and reduce unnecessary conflict.

Key words and phrases

long-term exclusion from the labour market
Being unable to find paid work for years.
asking for a narrow age profile
Wanting applicants only within a small age range.
combine recent training with practical judgement
Bring together up-to-date knowledge and experience-based decisions.
rely on stereotypes instead of evidence
Judge groups through assumptions rather than facts.
give both generations a fair chance
Offer equal consideration to younger and older workers.
