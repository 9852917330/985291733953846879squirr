News & Media

Why the news is mostly bad

Two-Part Question

Prompt

News plays an important part in most people's lives. Why is news so important to people? Why is so much news dedicated to bad news? Should the news focus on good news instead?

Model answer

Most people check the news before they have finished breakfast. It occupies that position because it tells us what has changed in a world we cannot see for ourselves, and it leans towards disaster for reasons that are partly commercial and partly human.

News matters because almost everything that affects a life is decided somewhere else. Interest rates, elections, war and epidemics all reach ordinary households, and knowing about them early allows people to act rather than react. It also makes citizenship possible, since a voter with no information is choosing blindly, and reporting is one of the few forces that makes powerful people uncomfortable. Beyond that, news gives a scattered population a shared set of events to discuss.

Bad news dominates for two reasons. The first is in us: human attention evolved to notice threats, so a report of danger is read while a report of steady improvement is scrolled past. The second is commercial, because outlets are paid by attention, and alarming headlines earn more of it. Bad events are also easier to report, since a bridge collapsing is a moment while a bridge standing safely for forty years is a process without a date.

Replacing this with good news would be a mistake, however. Reporting exists to warn and to hold power to account, and a diet of pleasant stories would leave the public unaware of what needs fixing. The honest correction is proportion rather than positivity: alongside each crisis, coverage should explain what is being done, how common the danger actually is, and what has improved, so that readers understand scale as well as drama.

In conclusion, news is important because it connects private lives to distant decisions, and it favours bad news because both our attention and the market reward it. The answer is more context and better proportion, not a cheerful substitute for reporting.

Key words and phrases

makes powerful people uncomfortable
Exposes facts influential people would prefer hidden.
a process without a date
An ongoing development without one clear event date.
understand scale as well as drama
Recognise the size of issues, not only excitement.
a cheerful substitute for reporting
Positive entertainment replacing factual journalism.
