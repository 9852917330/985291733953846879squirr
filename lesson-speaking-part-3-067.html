Money

Reacting to a situation and accounting for it

Part 3

Prompt

Do you think other people would feel the same in that situation? Have you had a similar experience since then? How would you account for the popularity of credit cards?

Do you think other people would feel the same in that situation?

Simple Model Answer

I think most people would feel embarrassed if they could not pay while others waited. The problem may be a technical fault rather than lack of money, but it can still feel as if people are judging you. A one-off incident is soon forgotten; if it connects with real money worries, the anxiety may last much longer.

Key words and phrases

one-off incident
Something that happens once rather than repeatedly.

Advanced Model Answer

Most people would, I think — the embarrassment comes from it happening in front of others rather than from the money itself. Being unable to pay in a queue makes you feel judged, even though the cause is usually something trivial, like a technical fault, rather than someone actually being broke. What varies is how long the feeling lasts, though: if it's a one-off, people forget it within the day, but if it reflects a genuine financial difficulty, the anxiety doesn't go away nearly as quickly.

Key words and phrases

how long the feeling lasts
the period for which that emotion continues
a genuine financial difficulty
a real problem caused by not having enough money

Have you had a similar experience since then?

Simple Model Answer

Yes, small payment problems have happened again, such as a card or app not working when I had no cash. They were not serious, but they changed my habits. I now carry some cash and another way to pay. It is mainly to avoid feeling stuck in front of a queue, rather than a concern about the amount.

Key words and phrases

another way to pay
A different payment method available if the first fails.

Advanced Model Answer

Minor versions, yes — a card that wouldn't read, or a payment app failing when I had no cash on me as a backup. Nothing serious, but enough to change my habits a little. I now carry a small amount of cash and keep a second payment method on me, which probably sounds excessive in a country where almost everyone pays by phone these days. It's less about the money, really, than about not wanting to be caught out in front of a queue of people.

Key words and phrases

a second payment method
another way to pay, such as an extra card

How would you account for the popularity of credit cards?

Simple Model Answer

Credit cards are convenient, widely accepted and useful abroad. Some also offer protection if a purchase goes wrong, along with rewards or cashback. However, paying later can make spending feel less real. If users leave balances unpaid, interest can become expensive. Their popularity is understandable, but the convenience works best when spending remains under control.

Key words and phrases

under control
Managed so it does not become excessive or harmful.

Advanced Model Answer

Convenience, mainly — they're accepted almost everywhere, work internationally, and mean you don't have to carry cash around. The protections matter too: if a purchase goes wrong, or a card's used fraudulently, you can usually get the money back, which you can't with cash, and rewards or cashback add a further incentive on top. The danger is that separating the purchase from the payment makes spending feel less real, and interest on unpaid balances is genuinely punishing.

Key words and phrases

get the money back
receive a refund
separating the purchase from the payment
making buying seem disconnected from spending money
punishing
very demanding or causing difficulty
