Competition

Competition in the classroom and prizes for children

Prompt

Why do you think some school teachers use competitions as class activities? Is it a good thing to give prizes to children who do well at school and why? Would you say that schools for young children have become more or less competitive since you were that age and why?

Why do you think some school teachers use competitions as class activities?

Simple Model Answer

Competitions can make a lesson more exciting and encourage children to join in. The same exercise may feel more enjoyable as a team game because pupils want to win. However, teachers need to be careful. Children who repeatedly lose may stop trying, so competition should support learning rather than make some students feel incapable.

Key words and phrases

join in
Take part in an activity with other people.

Advanced Model Answer

I think teachers use competitions mainly to motivate the children in their classes. I'm sure teachers try all kinds of activities to engage their pupils, and competitions might be one of the best ways to keep children interested or get them excited, because children love winning things and it changes the whole atmosphere of a lesson. A class that's gone completely quiet will suddenly come alive if you turn the same exercise into a team game, even though the content's identical. It doesn't always work, though — a child who never wins can switch off just as quickly as one who's fired up by it.

Key words and phrases

keep children interested
make sure children continue to pay attention
never wins
is never successful

Is it a good thing to give prizes to children who do well at school and why?

Simple Model Answer

Prizes may be useful in games or sport, but I would be careful about rewarding every good piece of schoolwork. Children should value learning itself. If they expect a prize each time, they may stop trying when rewards disappear. Occasional praise for effort seems more helpful than making academic work depend on winning something.

Key words and phrases

effort
The energy and care you put into doing something.

Advanced Model Answer

It might be a good idea to reward children for doing well in games or sports, but I don't think we should give prizes for their academic work. Children need to learn that the point of studying is to learn useful things that'll help them later in life, not to collect prizes. I've seen what happens when rewards become routine: a child who's used to getting a sticker every time stops trying the moment the stickers disappear, because the work itself was never the point. Occasional, low-key recognition for effort still seems far less risky than a formal prize system, though.

Key words and phrases

academic work
school or university assignments
low-key recognition
a quiet and modest way to praise someone

Would you say that schools for young children have become more or less competitive since you were that age and why?

Simple Model Answer

I think they have become more competitive. Testing starts earlier, parents compare results and children attend extra classes. This may improve marks for a while, but it can reduce curiosity and create anxiety. Parents also worry when other families do more, so it is difficult for one family to avoid the pressure alone.

Key words and phrases

curiosity
The desire to learn or understand new things.

Advanced Model Answer

Schools for young children do seem more competitive than they were in the past. Testing begins earlier, parents compare results more closely, and some children start extra classes before they've even developed a proper attitude to learning — I've seen five-year-olds doing maths worksheets after school simply because a neighbour's child is doing the same. That pressure can improve short-term performance, but it often comes at the cost of curiosity. Parents feel it too: they see other families paying for extra classes and worry their own child will fall behind, and that anxiety spreads fast, so it's very hard for one family to opt out alone.

Key words and phrases

fall behind
make less progress than others
opt out alone
be the only person to decide not to join
