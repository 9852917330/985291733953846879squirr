Advertising & Consumerism

Advertising aimed at children

Discuss Both Views

Prompt

A great deal of advertising is directed at children. Parents argue that children are misled by it, while advertisers claim that it is a useful source of information. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Model answer

Children are exposed to an enormous amount of advertising, and there is disagreement about whether this is harmful. Parents generally regard it as manipulation, while advertisers describe it as information. In my opinion the parents' concerns are better founded.

Advertisers do have a case. Children are consumers, and advertisements tell them what exists, what it costs and where it can be bought. Campaigns for milk, dental care, cycle helmets and road safety have all improved children's habits, and older children are far more sceptical than adults assume: most teenagers recognise exaggeration and dismiss it.

The objection, however, is that young children cannot. Below the age of about eight, children do not reliably understand that an advertisement is trying to sell them something, and they treat its claims as straightforward statements of fact. This makes it unreasonable to describe such advertising as information. Much of it also promotes products that are harmful in quantity, particularly sugary drinks, confectionery and fast food, and the rise in childhood obesity has followed it closely. A further problem is the pressure it places on families, since children who are told repeatedly that a toy or a brand of clothing is essential will demand it, and low-income parents are left choosing between debt and their child's disappointment.

In conclusion, I accept that advertising can inform older children, but the youngest are simply unable to evaluate what they are being shown. Advertising aimed at the under-tens, and all advertising for unhealthy food, should therefore be tightly restricted rather than left to the industry to regulate itself.

Key words and phrases

recognise exaggeration
Notice claims that overstate the truth.
straightforward statements of fact
Simple, direct descriptions of reality.
debt and their child's disappointment
Money owed and an unhappy child.
tightly restricted
Allowed only within strict limits.
