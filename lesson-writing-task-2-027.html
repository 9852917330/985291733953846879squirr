Crime & Punishment

Are criminals born or made?

Discuss Both Views

Prompt

Some scientists believe that a child's future criminal behaviour can be predicted from their behaviour at a very young age. To what extent is crime a product of human nature, and can children be prevented from growing up to be criminals?

Model answer

Research suggesting that difficult behaviour in very young children predicts later offending has led some people to conclude that criminals are born rather than made. In my opinion inherited temperament plays a real but limited part, and the environment in which a child grows up matters far more.

There is some evidence for the biological argument. Traits such as impulsiveness, aggression and an inability to feel guilt appear early, are partly inherited, and are found much more often among repeat offenders than in the general population. It would be dishonest to pretend that every child begins life with the same disposition.

However, a tendency is not a destiny. The great majority of impulsive or aggressive three-year-olds never commit a crime, and offending correlates far more strongly with poverty, neglect, family breakdown, poor schooling and drug addiction than with temperament. This is why crime rates differ so sharply between neighbourhoods and between countries, differences that genetics cannot possibly explain.

It follows that prevention is realistic. Programmes that support struggling parents, identify learning difficulties early, keep teenagers in education and provide activities in deprived areas have repeatedly been shown to reduce later offending, and they cost a small fraction of what imprisonment costs. A child with a difficult temperament who receives consistent guidance usually learns to control it.

In conclusion, human nature contributes to criminal behaviour but does not determine it. Since circumstances are what turn a difficult child into an offender, I believe most criminality can be prevented by acting early enough.

inability to feel guilt
Being unable to feel bad about wrongdoing.
a tendency is not a destiny

correlates far more strongly with

differences that genetics cannot possibly explain
