Animals & Wildlife

Why conservation has failed

Reasons / Solutions

Prompt

Far too little has been done to prevent animals and plants from dying out, although people have been aware of the problem for a long time. Why have people failed to improve this situation? What could be done about it?

Model answer

Although the loss of plant and animal species has been recognised for decades, remarkably little has been achieved. In my view this failure has two main causes, and there are practical steps that could improve the situation.

The first reason is economic. The activities that destroy habitats, such as logging, mining, intensive farming and industrial fishing, are extremely profitable and employ millions of people. A government that restricts them faces immediate opposition from companies and workers, whereas the benefits of conservation are distant and difficult to measure. The second reason is that responsibility is shared. Wildlife does not respect borders, so a country that protects a forest gains little if its neighbour clears one, and international agreements are usually voluntary and rarely enforced. No individual government therefore feels the loss directly, and there is little pressure from voters, most of whom now live in cities and never see the decline for themselves.

The most effective solution would be to make destruction expensive. Taxing timber, palm oil and fish that are produced unsustainably, and refusing to import them, would change commercial behaviour far faster than appeals to conscience. Wealthy countries should also pay poorer ones directly to maintain rainforests, since it is unreasonable to expect a low-income nation to forgo income for a global benefit that everybody else will share. Protected areas need proper funding for rangers and monitoring as well, because reserves that exist only on paper achieve nothing.

In conclusion, conservation has failed mainly because destruction is profitable and responsibility is divided. Progress therefore depends on financial penalties for those who damage habitats, and on genuine payments to the countries that agree to protect them.
