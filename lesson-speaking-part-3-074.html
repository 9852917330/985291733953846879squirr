Politeness

Showing politeness, at home and outside it

Prompt

In your country's culture, how do you show that you are being polite? Are we less polite with members of our families than with people we do not know?

In your country's culture, how do you show that you are being polite?

Simple Model Answer

In the UK, people often use polite questions, say please and thank you, and respect personal space. Waiting your turn in a queue is important too. These habits show consideration rather than direct demands. However, people from more direct-speaking cultures may misunderstand the careful wording as dishonest, when it is simply the usual local style.

Key words and phrases

consideration
Care for other people's feelings, needs or comfort.

Advanced Model Answer

Politeness in the UK's often shown through indirect language, personal space and frequent use of 'please', 'thank you' and 'sorry'. People normally say something like, 'Could we have the bill, please?' rather than giving a direct command — the wording softens the request and signals respect. Queuing's another one; people take it far more seriously than visitors often expect, and pushing in front of somebody is one of the few things that'll genuinely annoy an otherwise reserved crowd. The downside is that visitors from more direct-speaking cultures sometimes read all that indirectness as insincere, when it's really just the local convention.

Key words and phrases

softens the request
makes a request sound less demanding
signals respect
shows that you respect someone
the local convention
the usual practice in that community

Are we less polite with members of our families than with people we do not know?

Simple Model Answer

We usually speak more casually at home, using nicknames and language we might avoid with strangers. That does not mean respect disappears. In a family, politeness may show through remembering someone's needs or doing small favours rather than choosing formal words. I think the form changes because people know each other better, while the care can remain.

Key words and phrases

favours
Helpful actions done for someone without expecting payment.

Advanced Model Answer

I suppose it's normal to be a bit more relaxed about politeness with family members. Most people tend to speak in a more informal way at home — in the UK we still say 'please' and 'thanks', but it's fine to use colloquial language and nicknames you'd never use with someone you didn't know. I'd say the politeness doesn't actually disappear, though; it just changes form — within a family it shows up as remembering things and doing small favours rather than as careful wording.

Key words and phrases

more relaxed about politeness
less concerned about following formal rules of respect
speak in a more informal way
use everyday speech rather than formal words
changes form
appears in a different form
