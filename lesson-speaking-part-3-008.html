Books & Reading

Reading at speed, and jobs that involve heavy reading

Prompt

Are there any occasions when reading at speed is a useful skill? Are there any jobs in which people need to read a lot? Which ones? Do you think reading novels is more interesting than reading factual books, and why?

Are there any occasions when reading at speed is a useful skill?

Simple Model Answer

Yes, when you need to find one fact quickly or decide whether a document is useful. For example, you might scan a timetable, bill or long email. However, fast reading is less useful when you need a deep understanding. If I enjoy a book, I would rather take my time than rush through it.

Key words and phrases

take my time
Do something without hurrying.

Advanced Model Answer

Certainly, though I'd separate it from proper reading. Skimming's genuinely useful when you need one specific fact, or want to know whether a document's worth reading at all — bills, timetables, long work emails, the opening pages of a report. What it doesn't suit is anything you actually want to understand or enjoy. If a book's good, racing through it seems to me to defeat the entire point of having picked it up in the first place.

Key words and phrases

worth reading at all
Valuable enough to spend time reading.
defeat the entire point
make the original purpose impossible

Are there any jobs in which people need to read a lot? Which ones?

Simple Model Answer

Yes, lawyers, researchers and journalists read a great deal. They need to understand documents accurately because missing a detail can cause problems. Many office workers also spend hours reading emails and reports, although we don't usually call theirs reading jobs. It is often necessary reading rather than something they do to relax.

Key words and phrases

accurately
Correctly and with attention to important details.

Advanced Model Answer

Lawyers are the obvious example, since a single case can involve thousands of pages and missing one clause has real consequences. Researchers and academics read constantly, as do journalists and anyone working in policy. What's less obvious is that most ordinary office jobs now involve enormous amounts of reading — emails, reports, specifications. Nobody describes it as a reading job, but people spend hours a day doing exactly that, which says something about how narrowly we still define what counts as real work. It's just not the kind of reading anyone finds relaxing, which is probably why it doesn't get counted.

Key words and phrases

spend hours a day doing exactly that
devote several hours each day to precisely that activity

Do you think reading novels is more interesting than reading factual books, and why?

Simple Model Answer

Neither is always more interesting; they offer different experiences. A novel lets you imagine a world and understand its characters. A factual book explains real subjects, such as history or how the brain works. My choice depends on my mood. After a stressful week, I often choose a novel because facts can require more concentration.

Key words and phrases

factual book
A book about real information rather than an invented story.

Advanced Model Answer

I don't think either's inherently more interesting; they satisfy different appetites. A good novel creates a world convincing enough that the characters feel like people you know, and that's an experience non-fiction can't really provide. A well-written factual book gives you something quite different — a genuine insight into how something works, whether that's the human brain or an ancient civilisation. Which one appeals depends entirely on my mood, and on a stressful week I'll admit the novel usually wins, since fact-heavy books take more concentration than I've got left by the evening.

Key words and phrases

inherently more interesting
interesting by its very nature
a genuine insight into how something works
a real understanding of how a process operates
