Advice

Advice from friends or from family

Prompt

Is it better to get advice from a friend or from a family member? What would you say are the characteristics of a good adviser? Should people make their own work and career decisions, or is it a good idea to ask for advice about this?

Is it better to get advice from a friend or from a family member?

Simple Model Answer

It depends on the problem and the person. Older relatives may have useful experience, while a friend might discuss a worry more calmly without becoming too anxious. For example, I might discuss a money problem with a friend first. Still, some parents are very relaxed, so I would choose someone I trust rather than follow a fixed rule.

Key words and phrases

a fixed rule
An instruction that is expected to apply in every situation.

Advanced Model Answer

I think it depends on the kind of advice that you need. Parents and grandparents probably have more life experience than a friend, so you might get a wiser, more considered answer from them. Friends, on the other hand, are less likely to get anxious if you go to them with a problem — I probably wouldn't want to burden my parents with a financial worry, for instance, when a friend would just help me think it through calmly. It's not a hard rule, though; some parents are far more relaxed than some friends, so it really comes down to the individual person, not the category they fall into.

Key words and phrases

life experience
knowledge gained by living through events
burden my parents with
make my parents deal with a difficult responsibility

What would you say are the characteristics of a good adviser?

Simple Model Answer

A good adviser listens carefully before suggesting a solution. They ask questions to understand the situation and help you think for yourself. My aunt does this: she asks questions until I work out my own answer. They should also accept that their experience may not fit every problem, instead of acting certain about everything.

Key words and phrases

work out my own answer
Find a solution by thinking for myself.

Advanced Model Answer

I'd say the most important quality is being a good listener — someone who takes the time to actually understand the situation instead of jumping straight to a solution. That matters because most people who ask for advice have already half-decided what they want to hear, so a good adviser needs to ask the right questions rather than just handing out opinions. My aunt's like this: she almost never tells you what to do, she just asks questions until you work it out yourself, which is far more useful than being told outright. The one limitation is that even a good adviser can only draw on their own experience, so it's worth being wary of anyone who's certain their solution fits every situation.

Key words and phrases

a good listener
someone who pays attention and understands when others speak
ask the right questions
choose questions that help reveal useful answers

Should people make their own work and career decisions, or is it a good idea to ask for advice about this?

Simple Model Answer

People should ask for advice, but make the final choice themselves. Family or colleagues may notice a risk or opportunity they have missed. However, advice can reflect the adviser's priorities, such as wanting a secure job. The person choosing must consider that and take responsibility, because they will live with the result.

Key words and phrases

take responsibility
Accept that you are answerable for a decision and its effects.

Advanced Model Answer

I'd say it's a combination of both. Most people benefit from discussing career choices with family, teachers or experienced colleagues, because someone outside the situation often notices risks or opportunities you've missed yourself. I know someone who almost turned down a job abroad because her parents were nervous about the move, and it turned out to be the best decision she's made. That said, the final decision has to rest with the individual, who must take ultimate responsibility for the consequences. Advice is also worth weighing by where it comes from — parents often prize security above everything, which is understandable but can just as easily steer someone away from a risk that was actually worth taking.

Key words and phrases

take ultimate responsibility for the consequences
accept final responsibility for what happens as a result
prize security above everything
value feeling safe above all else
