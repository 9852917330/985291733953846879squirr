Consumerism & Possessions

What shows status now and in your grandparents' time

Part 3

Prompt

What kinds of possessions show status in your country? Do you think it was different for your grandparents? Modern society is often called 'materialistic'. Why do you think this is?

What kinds of possessions show status in your country?

Simple Model Answer

Homes, cars, expensive watches, branded clothes and new phones can all show status. They work only because other people recognise them as signs of success. A costly watch may not tell the time better, for example. These signals change quickly, and some younger people now prefer showing experiences, such as holidays, rather than possessions.

Key words and phrases

branded clothes
Clothing sold under a recognised company or designer name.

Advanced Model Answer

Property first, then cars, then branded clothing, watches and the latest phone. What's interesting is that the signal only works because everyone agrees it does — an expensive watch keeps time no better than a cheap one, so the entire value is social. The specific items shift quite quickly, too: something that signalled success ten years ago can look dated now, which is precisely what keeps people buying replacements they don't really need. That's starting to shift a little, though — plenty of younger buyers now signal status through experiences instead of objects, a good holiday rather than a good watch.

Key words and phrases

the entire value is social
it is worth something because other people value or notice it
experiences instead of objects
memories and activities rather than physical belongings

Do you think it was different for your grandparents?

Simple Model Answer

Yes. My grandparents valued things that lasted, such as land, gold and strong furniture, because possessions were harder to obtain and offered security. Today, people replace things faster and show purchases online to a much wider audience. Their generation had less pressure of that kind, but also fewer choices, so I would not say everything was better.

Key words and phrases

security
A feeling of safety and protection from future difficulties.

Advanced Model Answer

Yes, considerably. Their generation valued things that lasted — land, gold, solid furniture — partly because goods were genuinely scarce and partly because owning something durable meant security. Status came from what you had accumulated over a lifetime. Now it's much more visible and much faster-moving, largely because social media displays everyone's purchases continuously: my grandparents' neighbours knew what they owned, and that was the whole audience, whereas the audience is effectively unlimited now. I wouldn't call their era better exactly, though — they also had far less choice and far less means of fixing a bad purchase once they'd made it.

Key words and phrases

what you had accumulated over a lifetime
all the things you had collected during your life
the audience is effectively unlimited
almost anyone could see it

Modern society is often called 'materialistic'. Why do you think this is?

Simple Model Answer

Modern economies depend on people continuing to buy things, so businesses strongly encourage spending. Adverts link products with identity, while social media lets us compare ourselves with many others. This can make possessions seem unusually important. However, some people resist by owning less and choosing carefully, so not everyone accepts the same values.

Key words and phrases

identity
The qualities and ideas that form who a person is.

Advanced Model Answer

Partly because economies now depend on continuous consumption — if people stopped replacing things, a huge amount of employment would disappear, so there's enormous pressure to keep buying. Advertising then does the work of linking products with identity, so you're not purchasing a phone but a version of yourself. Comparison has widened, too: people once measured themselves against neighbours, and now it's against everyone they follow online. A minority of people do consciously resist that pressure, though, and minimalism as a lifestyle has grown partly in response to it.

Key words and phrases

linking products with identity
making a product seem part of who you are
resist that pressure
avoid giving in to that influence
