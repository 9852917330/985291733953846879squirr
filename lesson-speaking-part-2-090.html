Object & Favourite

A TV programme or channel that you enjoy

Part 2

Prompt

Describe a TV programme or channel that you enjoy

You should say: - What it is called and what it was about? - When do you usually watch it - Why do you prefer it to other programmes or channels?

Simple Model Answer

A programme source I enjoy is TED. It is a website rather than a normal television channel, but I use it to watch short talks whenever I want. The talks cover subjects such as science, education, health and society.

I have the website saved on my phone and check it every few days. A short presentation often fits well into my train journey to work. I like hearing an expert explain an unfamiliar idea in clear language. One talk about sleep and memory encouraged me to stop working so late at night, so it had a practical effect on my routine.

The main advantage is learning something useful in a short time. However, a brief talk cannot explain every side of a complicated issue. I try to remember that a confident speaker may still be simplifying the subject. If an idea seems important, I read more afterwards. For me, TED is a good way to discover questions and interests, rather than a replacement for studying a topic in greater detail.

Key words and phrases

practical effect
A change that influences real actions or everyday life.
simplifying the subject
Making a topic easier to explain by leaving out some details.

Advanced Model Answer

I rarely watch traditional television channels nowadays; I much prefer searching for interesting programmes or videos online. So, although it isn't a conventional TV channel, I'd like to talk about TED, a website that functions like a modern on-demand channel. TED stands for Technology, Entertainment and Design, and it features short talks on subjects ranging from science and education to health and social change.

The main advantage is that I can watch the talks whenever I want. I've got the website saved on my phone, and I usually check it every few days. I often watch one presentation during my train journey to work because most talks are concise enough to fit into a short commute.

I prefer TED to ordinary television because I learn something useful almost every time. Its slogan, “ideas worth spreading”, captures its appeal perfectly. Instead of spending an hour on a repetitive talk show, I can watch a specialist explain a breakthrough in medicine, a new teaching method or an unfamiliar social issue in ten or fifteen minutes. The talk I remember best was one on how sleep affects memory. The speaker was a neuroscientist, but she explained the research so clearly that I changed my own habits within a week, and I've never gone back to working late the way I used to.

If I had to name a drawback, it'd be that the format encourages simplification. A complicated subject compressed into twelve minutes inevitably loses its qualifications, and the confident delivery can make an idea sound more settled than it really is. So I treat the talks as a starting point rather than the final word, and I look things up afterwards if a claim seems important.
