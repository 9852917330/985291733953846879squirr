Children & Family

The end of the extended family

Positive or Negative

Prompt

In many nations, people in huge cities live alone or in small family units instead of in large, extended family groups. Is this a positive or negative trend?

Model answer

Households in large cities have shrunk from several generations to two people or one. The change brings genuine freedom, but I regard it as negative overall because of what it removes from the young and the old at the same time.

The advantages are easy to identify. Living apart from an extended family allows people to choose their work, their partner and their beliefs without constant supervision, and it has freed women in particular from obligations that were assumed rather than chosen. Small households reduce friction, since disagreements between generations about money, religion and child-rearing are among the commonest sources of unhappiness. Practically, small units also move easily, which matters where work requires it, and a couple can accept a job in another city without negotiating with a dozen relatives.

The losses are heavier than they first appear. Extended families provided childcare, so young parents now pay for what was once shared, and the cost keeps many from having children at all. They also cared for the elderly, and their absence has produced widespread loneliness among older people and a growing bill for residential care that states struggle to meet. Children lose the daily presence of grandparents, from whom they learn a longer view of life. A small household is fragile too, because illness, redundancy or divorce leaves nobody to absorb the shock the way a larger group could.

There is a wider social effect. Cities of solitary households consume more housing, energy and goods per person, and loneliness has become common enough among young adults to appear in health statistics.

In conclusion, smaller households have given people freedom that most would not surrender. The trend is nonetheless negative, because it has transferred care from families to money, and many households cannot afford the exchange.

Key words and phrases

transferred care from families to money
Replaced relatives' unpaid care with paid services.
