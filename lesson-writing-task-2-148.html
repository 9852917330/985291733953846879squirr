Advertising & Consumerism

Do high sales reflect advertising or real need?

Agree / Disagree

Prompt

The high sales of popular consumer goods reflect the power of advertising rather than the real needs of the society in which they are sold. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Model answer

It is sometimes argued that the popularity of consumer goods demonstrates the power of advertising rather than any genuine social need. I largely agree with this view, although advertising cannot create demand entirely on its own.

The evidence for the argument is easy to find. Many of the products that sell in the greatest quantities meet no need that previously existed. Bottled water is the clearest example, since it is sold at hundreds of times the price of tap water of comparable quality, and its market was created almost entirely by marketing. The annual replacement of phones and clothing follows the same pattern: the previous model still works perfectly, but advertising has redefined it as embarrassing. Enormous sums are involved, and companies would not continue spending them year after year if they did not measurably shift behaviour.

Advertising is also particularly effective where a product is difficult to judge. With cosmetics, supplements and branded clothing, buyers cannot easily assess quality, so image determines the decision, which is why these industries advertise most heavily.

Nevertheless, the claim can be pushed too far. Advertising cannot sustain a product that people dislike, and expensive campaigns for genuinely poor products fail regularly. Consumers also buy a great many things, such as washing machines and medicines, simply because they solve real problems, and these would sell with or without promotion.

In conclusion, I agree that advertising rather than necessity explains much of what sells in large quantities. It works, however, by directing existing desires rather than by inventing them from nothing.

Key words and phrases

meet no need that previously existed
Serve needs people did not previously have.
created almost entirely by marketing
Produced mainly by promotional activity.
redefined it as embarrassing
Made people see it as shameful.
measurably shift behaviour
Change actions in observable ways.
the claim can be pushed too far
The argument can become an exaggeration.
