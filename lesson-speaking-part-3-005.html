Advice

Learning from other people's experience

Part 3

Prompt

Does that person still give you advice? Is it helpful to learn from other people's experiences? What kind of advice do parents give to their children?

Does that person still give you advice?

Simple Model Answer

Yes, but I ask for a different kind of help now. When I was younger, I wanted instructions. Now I want another opinion about risks I might have missed, such as when I considered changing jobs. That leaves the choice with me. Sometimes I still want a quick answer, but usually discussion is more helpful.

Key words and phrases

another opinion
A different person's view of a situation.

Advanced Model Answer

Yes, though the nature of it's changed completely. When I was younger I'd ask what to do and just expect an answer. Now I ask more for perspective rather than instructions — when I was deciding whether to change jobs last year, for example, I didn't want to be told what to pick, I wanted to know what they'd be worried about, or what I might not be seeing. That's more useful, because the decision stays mine. It doesn't always work, though — sometimes I still just want someone to tell me the answer, and perspective isn't much comfort when you're exhausted and need a decision made quickly. But mostly they've adapted too, offering far less unsolicited advice than they used to.

Key words and phrases

perspective rather than instructions
a wider way to see the issue, without orders
unsolicited advice
suggestions given when nobody asked for them

Is it helpful to learn from other people's experiences?

Simple Model Answer

Yes, it can save us from repeating mistakes. Someone who has already done something can warn us about problems; a friend studying abroad, for example, might explain visa delays. However, their situation may differ from ours. I would use their experience as useful information, rather than assume I must do exactly what they did.

Key words and phrases

assume
Believe something is true without checking it fully.

Advanced Model Answer

Definitely, because otherwise everyone has to make every mistake personally, which is a slow and expensive way to learn. Somebody who's already done what you're attempting can warn you about the predictable problems — a friend who'd already done a master's abroad warned me about visa delays I'd never have thought to plan for. The limitation is that circumstances differ: advice that worked twenty years ago in a different economy might not transfer at all, and people tend to over-generalise from their own case. So I treat experience as evidence rather than instruction.

Key words and phrases

the predictable problems
difficulties you can reasonably expect
over-generalise from their own case
draw a broad conclusion based only on their own experience

What kind of advice do parents give to their children?

Simple Model Answer

Parents often advise children about school, money, relationships and safety because these affect their future. Young children need simple instructions, such as looking before crossing a road. As children grow, parents can explain choices and ask questions instead. The right approach depends on the child, since some teenagers still want direct help with important decisions.

Key words and phrases

direct help
Clear guidance about what to do.

Advanced Model Answer

Mostly about education, money, relationships and personal safety — the areas where a mistake has lasting consequences. What I've noticed is that good advice changes shape as children grow. With a young child it's fairly direct instruction, because they can't assess risk themselves — 'don't cross without looking,' not a discussion. With a teenager the same parent has to shift towards asking questions rather than issuing rules, otherwise it simply gets ignored. That shift doesn't happen at the same pace for every family, though — some teenagers still want, and get, fairly direct guidance on the big decisions, like which subject to study.

Key words and phrases

asking questions rather than issuing rules
helping people think for themselves instead of commanding them
