An important skill which cannot be learned at school

Part 2

Prompt

Describe an important skill which cannot be learned at school

You should say: - What the skill is - Why it cannot be learned at school - Why it is important - How we can learn it

Simple Model Answer

An important skill I did not learn properly at school is public speaking. I developed it later through presentation classes at an English centre. A mentor called Jimmy helped me understand how to speak more confidently in front of people.

During my first attempt, I read from a complete script in a nervous voice. After about a minute, I lost my place and stopped. Jimmy noticed that I had avoided looking at anyone. He asked me to try again while looking at him, which made the task feel more manageable.

I now use presentations at work, including when explaining quarterly results. I still follow his advice by choosing a few friendly faces in the room and speaking towards them. Learning took about a year of regular practice and feedback. I think this skill matters because knowing information is not enough if you cannot explain it clearly to others. Books can offer advice, but real improvement comes from actually speaking, noticing problems and trying again. It is a practical ability I continue to develop.

Advanced Model Answer

The skill I want to talk about is public speaking — specifically, giving presentations — which schools don't really teach, even though almost every job needs it eventually. I picked up most of it from a mentor, Jimmy, who ran presentation classes at the English centre I studied at a few years ago.

Schools teach facts and grammar rules, but they rarely put you in front of a room and force you to hold people's attention, which is really the only way to improve. I remember my first attempt under Jimmy's guidance: I'd written out my whole talk and read it off the page in a shaky voice, and about a minute in I completely lost my place and stood there blushing while twenty classmates waited. It was excruciatingly awkward, but Jimmy stopped the session, pointed out I'd lost eye contact the second I got nervous, and had me try again looking only at him.

It matters because in almost any job you eventually have to stand up and explain something to a room full of people, whether that's a project update or convincing a client to sign off on an idea. I've used it constantly since — I now present quarterly figures at work, and I still use Jimmy's trick of picking three friendly faces in the room and speaking mainly to them.

There's no real shortcut to learning it — you have to put yourself in front of people repeatedly and get feedback each time, whether that's a course, a Toastmasters group, or recording yourself and watching it back. It took about a year of doing that regularly before speaking in front of a group stopped making my hands shake.
