Luck & Superstition

Superstition and what people believe

Part 3

Prompt

Do you think people in your country are superstitious? Can you describe a superstition that they believe in? Do you think people are less superstitious than they used to be?

Do you think people in your country are superstitious?

Simple Model Answer

Some are, especially around dates, numbers and important decisions. Older people may take these beliefs more literally, while younger people often follow them as traditions. However, superstition can affect any age when the result matters and feels outside our control. In those moments, a familiar custom may provide comfort even when someone doubts that it really works.

Key words and phrases

superstition
A belief that certain actions or objects bring luck without a clear logical basis.

Advanced Model Answer

To a degree, yes, though it varies a lot by generation. Older people tend to take certain beliefs seriously, particularly around dates, numbers and important decisions, whereas younger people tend to treat the same customs as tradition rather than literal belief — they'll follow them at a wedding without genuinely thinking that ignoring them would cause misfortune. It's not strictly an age thing, though; what I've noticed is that superstition strengthens whenever the stakes are high and the outcome's outside your control, whatever generation you're from.

Key words and phrases

literal belief
believing it is physically or exactly true

Can you describe a superstition that they believe in?

Simple Model Answer

Beliefs about lucky and unlucky numbers are common. Their sounds can suggest positive or negative meanings in the language. People may pay extra for a particular phone number or avoid certain floor numbers. Some admit there is no logical evidence, but still prefer following the custom rather than feeling they have taken an unnecessary chance.

Key words and phrases

logical evidence
Facts or reasoning that clearly support an idea.

Advanced Model Answer

The most widespread one's probably to do with numbers. Certain numbers are considered lucky and others unlucky, largely because of how they sound in the language, and it has real practical consequences: people will pay significantly more for a phone number or a house number with the right digits, and buildings sometimes skip unlucky floor numbers entirely. What's interesting is that plenty of the people who do this will happily admit there's no rational basis for it — they just don't want to take the risk.

Key words and phrases

skip unlucky floor numbers
leave out floor labels people think are unlucky
no rational basis
no sound reason supported by evidence

Do you think people are less superstitious than they used to be?

Simple Model Answer

Probably fewer people believe a ritual directly changes an outcome, because education offers more scientific explanations. However, the customs have not disappeared. They may now provide comfort or a sense of tradition. Students and athletes can follow routines before important events without fully believing in them, simply because doing so makes them feel less anxious.

Key words and phrases

ritual
A repeated action performed in a particular way for a special reason.

Advanced Model Answer

Less so in the literal sense, yes — education and access to scientific explanation mean fewer people genuinely believe a ritual will change an outcome. But it hasn't disappeared so much as changed function; it survives as cultural habit and emotional comfort more than actual belief. You see it clearly with students before exams, or athletes with pre-match routines. They don't really believe it works; it just reduces anxiety when they can't control what happens next.

Key words and phrases

changed function
now serves a different purpose
pre-match routines
usual actions done before a game begins
