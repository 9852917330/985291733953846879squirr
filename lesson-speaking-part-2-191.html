Situation

Something you did that made you feel proud of yourself

Part 2

Prompt

Describe something you did that made you feel proud of yourself.

You should say: - what you did - when you did it - why you felt proud of what you had done - and explain how you felt when this happened.

Simple Model Answer

Something I did that made me proud was completing my undergraduate degree about ten years ago. The clearest moment was opening my final results and discovering that I had done better than I expected.

The course involved difficult essays, presentations and group projects. My family supported me, but I still had to manage my own time and motivation. During the final term, I spent many evenings in the library and learned to divide large assignments into smaller stages. That made the work easier to handle.

When I saw the result, I did not immediately celebrate loudly. I sat quietly for a moment and then called my parents. My mother cried, which made me realise how much the achievement meant to her too. I felt proud because the degree came from years of effort rather than luck or one successful exam. It also created opportunities for further study and employment. Most importantly, I learned that I could continue working towards something uncertain. I still remember that lesson when I begin a long project and progress feels slow.

Key words and phrases

undergraduate degree
A first university qualification, usually completed before a Master's degree.
employment
Paid work or the state of having a job.

Advanced Model Answer

One of my proudest achievements was completing my undergraduate degree. The moment I remember most clearly is opening my final results and realising I'd done better than I'd expected.

I graduated about ten years ago after several genuinely demanding years of study. The course was full of difficult assignments, presentations and group projects, so that final result meant a lot more than just passing a set of exams, it showed I'd actually developed the discipline to manage long-term work and keep going even when progress felt painfully slow.

My family supported me the whole way through, but I still had to take responsibility for my own schedule and motivation. During the final term especially, I spent countless evenings in the library, and that's really where I learned to break large tasks down into smaller, more manageable stages.

I felt proud because that degree was the first major goal I'd ever achieved through sustained effort, rather than luck or last-minute cramming. It also opened doors, both for further study and for work, so its value wasn't just personal, it was practical too. The moment itself, though, was oddly quiet, not at all what I'd imagined. I'd pictured some big celebration, but when the results actually came up on screen, I just sat there for a minute before calling my parents. My mum ended up crying, which honestly surprised me more than the grade itself did.

What I really took away from it, in the end, wasn't the qualification at all. It was discovering that I could commit to something for three whole years without any guarantee it would work out, and still see it through. I've drawn on that more than once since, any time I've started something where the outcome was uncertain and progress was slow.
