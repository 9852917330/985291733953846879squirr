Consumerism & Possessions

Possessions as a mark of status

Prompt

In your country, what possessions show a higher status or position in society? Do you think we place too much importance on possessions? Do you think things were different 50 years ago?

In your country, what possessions show a higher status or position in society?

Simple Model Answer

Homes and land are the clearest signs, followed by expensive cars, watches and designer clothes. These things show status because other people can see them and recognise their price. Phones used to do this too, but they are now so common that they show less. The particular products change as fashions and ideas of success change.

Key words and phrases

status
A person's social position or level of respect in society.

Advanced Model Answer

Property first — where you live and whether you own it says more than anything else. Then cars, luxury watches and designer clothing, and until recently the latest phone, though phones have become so universal now that they don't signal much any more. What these have in common is visibility: a possession only works as a status marker if other people can see it and recognise what it cost, which is exactly why the specific items shift so quickly — today's must-have brand is often forgotten in five years.

Key words and phrases

visibility
the extent to which something is seen or noticed
recognise what it cost
understand how much effort or money it required

Do you think we place too much importance on possessions?

Simple Model Answer

Yes, I think adverts and social media encourage us to connect possessions with who we are. We compare ourselves with more people than before, and teenagers may feel pressure to wear certain brands. That can be hard for families with less money. Still, some people choose to ignore this competition and focus on what they actually need.

Key words and phrases

possessions
The things a person owns.

Advanced Model Answer

Yes, though I'd say the pressure's manufactured rather than natural. Advertising works precisely by linking products to identity, so you're not buying a jacket but a version of yourself, and social media has widened the comparison enormously. It's hardest on teenagers, who face genuine social consequences for having the wrong brand — being excluded over a pair of trainers is a real thing — and whose families often can't afford to prevent it. Not everyone falls for it equally, though; I know plenty of people who've simply opted out of the whole game.

Key words and phrases

a version of yourself
a particular image of the person you are
genuine social consequences
real effects on relationships with other people
opted out of the whole game
chosen not to take part at all

Do you think things were different 50 years ago?

Simple Model Answer

Yes. People owned less and often repaired things because new goods were expensive and borrowing money was harder. My grandfather remembers repairing his shoes instead of replacing them. Advertising was also less constant. People still cared about possessions, but buying newer versions happened more slowly than it often does now.

Key words and phrases

replacing
Getting something new to take the place of an older item.

Advanced Model Answer

Considerably. People owned far fewer things and got them repaired instead of replacing them, because goods were expensive relative to income and credit was much harder to obtain — you generally couldn't buy what you hadn't saved for. My grandfather still talks about having his shoes resoled rather than buying new ones, which sounds almost unthinkable now. Advertising existed then too, but it reached you a few times a day rather than constantly. Materialism isn't new, but the cycle of upgrading has accelerated beyond anything he'd recognise, which says as much about marketing as it does about human nature.

Key words and phrases

the cycle of upgrading
repeatedly buying newer versions
