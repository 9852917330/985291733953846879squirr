Simple Model Answer

An interesting talk I heard was The Power of Vulnerability by Brene Brown. She is an American researcher and writer. I came across the talk on television about two months ago after a difficult day at work.

One idea caught my attention: when we try to avoid painful feelings completely, we may also make it harder to experience happiness and gratitude. She connected this with being willing to admit mistakes, ask for help and allow other people to see our worries.

I recognised some of my own behaviour in her examples. I often said I was fine even when I was struggling because I did not want to seem weak. Since watching, I have tried to be more honest in small conversations with friends. I liked the talk because she combined research with personal stories, including experiences that were embarrassing for her. This made the message easier to understand and believe. I still think about it when I need support. It reminded me that being open about a problem can be a form of courage rather than a failure.

Key words and phrases

Vulnerability
Being open about feelings or difficulties even when this makes you feel exposed.
gratitude
A feeling of being thankful.

Advanced Model Answer

I'm going to talk about a talk I came across on TV about two months ago called The Power of Vulnerability. It's a TED talk given by Brene Brown, an American researcher and author who's spent years studying a range of topics, including vulnerability, courage, shame and empathy.

I stumbled across it completely by accident. I'd had a pretty rough day at work and was just flicking through channels at home when I landed on it, and within about two minutes I was completely hooked. What really got to me was the way she talked about how we all try to numb difficult feelings — and how, when you do that, you end up numbing the good feelings too, like joy and gratitude, not just the bad ones.

That idea properly stuck with me, because it described exactly what I'd been doing for years without realising it — avoiding anything that made me feel exposed, whether that was admitting I was wrong about something or telling a friend I actually needed help. Since watching it, I've genuinely tried to be more open, even in small ways, like being honest when I'm struggling instead of just saying I'm fine.

I found the talk so interesting because it wasn't full of vague advice — she backed everything up with years of her own research, but she also told really personal, sometimes embarrassing stories about herself, which made the whole thing feel honest rather than preachy. It's the kind of talk I still think about whenever I'm having a bad day.
